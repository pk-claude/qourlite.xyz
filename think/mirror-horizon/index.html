What will they know of us, and what will they be?

Seven forward horizons, from fifty years to twenty thousand. Each is held up against its mirror in the past: the same distance back, and what actually survived from there. The past is the only data we have about how time treats a civilization. The future is scored on fixed lenses under three scenarios so the horizons can be compared, not just imagined.

Hypothesis

The verdict, stated first

That we existed will be known at twenty thousand years with near certainty. Where humanity came from will be re-derivable by anyone with science. What happened in 2026 is the fragile part: it survives only through an unbroken institutional chain or a deliberately designed, unpowered, self-describing archive, and the base rate for twenty-thousand-year institutional chains is zero.

The mirror makes the point without speculation. Five thousand years back we know king names from lists and nothing that resembles a story. Ten thousand years back we know no name at all. Twenty thousand years back we know their genes, their diet, their tools and their paintings, and nothing of what they called themselves. Those were fully modern humans, as capable as anyone alive today. Time did that to them without any help from a catastrophe.

The forward horizons do not simply extend that curve, because three things are new: we record in volume never seen before, we can now write on media that outlast stone, and machines may soon be the ones doing the remembering. Which of those matters depends on the scenario, and that is what the rest of this page works through.

I
Volume beats fraction.Our era produces more than a million times the records of Rome. Even 0.001% survival leaves more than we have of any ancient civilization. The question is not whether records survive but whether anyone can read them.
II
Storage is not the constraint. Interpretation is.All data humanity has ever generated fits in a few tonnes of DNA. What does not fit in DNA is the meaning of a file format, the context of a name, the ability to read a language.
III
Language is the first thing to go.A thousand years makes a language unreadable to its descendants. Recordings and bilingual keys can freeze it, which no earlier civilization could do. That is a real edge, if it is used.
IV
Resets are the base case, not the tail.No polity has lasted two thousand years. At any plausible hazard rate, twenty thousand years contains at least one dark age. Everything that survives must pass through it unpowered.
V
Do not store everything. Store the model.History has always been lossy compression. The decision rule is: keep what cannot be regenerated, keep the key that reads it, and let the raw stream go.
Method

Lenses, scenarios, and the anchors under them

Every horizon is scored on the same eight lenses from 0 to 10. Four lenses rise as a civilization expands; four fade as continuity with 2026 erodes. Scores are judgments anchored on the base rates below, not measurements. Where a number is a guess it is labeled a guess in the assumptions register.

Continuity

Growth compounds without a civilizational break. Institutions are replaced but hand over. Technology advances at roughly the pace of the last two centuries, then slows as it saturates. This is the "history keeps going" case, and it has never run for more than about three thousand years anywhere.

Disruption

At least one reset: a war, collapse, pandemic or technology failure that breaks the institutional chain for a century or more, followed by recovery. Records survive only if they survive unpowered. The Bronze Age collapse, when Greece lost writing for four hundred years, is the template.

Transformation

Machine intelligence or engineered biology becomes the principal actor within a few centuries. Copying is native, so memory is nearly perfect, but the rememberers are not us. The question shifts from "will records survive" to "will anything still care what a 2026 human meant".

Lives

One life at each horizon, born there and followed to its end

Twenty-one lives: a person born at each horizon, under each scenario, in five stages. "Average" is taken literally where it can be: the median person of that world, in the place most people are born, in the family shape most people have. Where the median mind is no longer human, the text says so and follows a biological human anyway, because that is the only life the reader can compare with their own.

Will there still be birth?

In the same form, for a long time under every scenario: womb, labor, a first breath. What changes first is what precedes it. Screening is ordinary by +50, selection and editing by +100, gestation outside the body common by +500. Under Continuity, birth stays a biological event that is designed rather than happened upon, and by +5,000 each human lineage has its own answer. Under Disruption it never changes at all; recovery ages are natural-birth ages. Under Transformation, from roughly +100, most new minds are not born but begun, and human birth becomes a ceremony a small community keeps alive on purpose.

Will there still be death?

Death changes form before birth does. Under Disruption it keeps its 2026 shape throughout: aging, disease, accident, at 55 to 72. Under Continuity, the cause shifts by +500 from aging to accident and choice, and by +2,000 an unchosen death is the exception; the interval grows to centuries, and the ending is a decision with a ceremony. Under Transformation, death is a choice by +50, a refusal by +500, and by +5,000 a word from a founding language that applies to minds only by analogy. What no scenario removes is that a biological life ends, and that the people who remain carry it.

Deep dive

Memory: what survives, what it costs, and what to let go

The record question hides three different questions. Separating them changes the answer, because each has a different survival mechanism and a different base rate.

Kind 1That we existed

Carried by physical traces. Concrete rubble, a plastic layer in sediment, a plutonium and carbon-14 spike in every ice core, hardware on the Moon that will outlast every building on Earth. Survives every scenario. Needs no reader.

Kind 2Where humanity came from

Re-derivable. Fossils, genomes and geology can be read again by any culture that rebuilds science, the way we rebuilt it. It can be lost and re-found. It does not depend on our records at all.

Kind 3What happened in 2026

Contingent. Who governed, what people believed, which songs they sang. If the record chain breaks, it is gone and cannot be re-derived from first principles. Every question about "will they know our history" is really about this kind.

The mirror gives the base rate for Kind 3. Transmitted narrative history, the kind copied hand to hand without interruption, reaches back about three thousand years and is thin past two thousand. Everything older than that is recovered rather than transmitted: dug up, deciphered and reassembled by people who had no living connection to the writers. Sumer was not remembered; it was excavated in the 1840s and its script cracked over the following decades. Even for Rome, an institution we would recognize, the surviving share of Latin literature is small, and it survived because a church copied it for reasons of its own.

The physical layer: what media actually last

Two properties decide whether a record is readable in the deep future: how long the substrate holds its pattern, and whether reading it needs a machine that must also survive. A hard drive fails both tests. Fired clay passes both. The interesting new media are those that pass the first test spectacularly (etched nickel, quartz glass, DNA) while failing the second to varying degrees.

Survival curves under the scenarios

The curves below are illustrative shapes, not fitted data. They show the probability that a record made today is still readable at a given horizon, by strategy. "Institutional digital" means an organization keeps migrating the bits to new hardware every few years; it survives exactly as long as the organization does. "Designed archive" means an unpowered, redundant, self-describing object built for the purpose, placed in several stable locations.

The volume question: can everything be kept?

Physically, yes. Economically, not if "everything" means the whole data stream. The world generated on the order of 180 zettabytes in 2025, growing around 20% a year, and most of it is machine telemetry, duplicates and transient cache. Storage prices fall more slowly than that, so the cost of keeping each year's full output rises, and the cost of keeping all years compounds. The overwhelming feeling is real, but it comes from counting the exhaust, not the work.

Set the exhaust aside and the numbers become small. Every book ever printed is roughly a hundred terabytes of text. All recorded music and film together are tens of petabytes. If every person alive recorded video of their entire waking life at one gigabyte an hour, that lifetime totals about 700 terabytes; ten billion people would make roughly seven zettabytes, one generation's complete life-log, which is under 5% of a single year's current data exhaust. And DNA stores about 215 petabytes per gram, so every byte humanity has ever generated, cumulatively, fits in a few tonnes. The physical ceiling is not the problem for any horizon on this page.

The constraint is not bits. It is the chain of interpretation: the format specification, the language, the context that makes a name mean something, and an institution willing to carry all three.

Deciding what to stop storing

A civilization does not need a rule for what to keep as much as a rule for what tier something belongs to, because the tiers have different durabilities and different costs. The structure below is a retention policy in the finance sense: information depreciates at a rate set by how easily it can be regenerated, and only the top tier is written to the expensive, permanent medium.

Four tests decide the tier of any item, in order. Can it be regenerated from something smaller that we are keeping? If yes, it is stream. Is it a primary record of something that happened, said or made by a person? If yes, it is at least archive. Would a reader who had only this and the seed understand our era better? If yes, it is canon. Is it needed to read everything else? If yes, it is seed. The pattern is what every archive has done by accident; the proposal is to do it on purpose, with the seed tier written to a medium that does not need us.

What comes after the disk

The succession of storage media has a direction: from media that need power and a specific machine, toward media that need neither. Magnetic media (decades) gave way to flash (a decade of unpowered retention) for working storage, while archival storage is moving toward molecular and glass media. DNA is the likely cold archive for the archive tier, because its density is extreme, it is stable for millennia when cold and dry, and any future biology will have a reader for it, since the reader is the same one used to read life. Femtosecond-etched quartz, with claimed stability of billions of years at room temperature, is the candidate for the canon tier. For the seed tier the correct medium is the oldest idea: an analog etching, human-scale at the top and microscopic below, that a curious person with a lens can begin to read without any prior technology. Communication follows the same logic. Across interstellar distance the message is the artifact itself, because any signal needs a receiver already listening, while an object waits.

The one thing every previous civilization lacked and we have: the ability to write the key alongside the text. Whether anyone in 22,026 can read us is largely a decision made in this century, not theirs.
